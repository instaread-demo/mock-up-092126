Sentence Case vs. Title Case

  • Save for later

Should this heading be written in sentence case or title case?

Both can be correct. The right choice depends on what you are writing, who will read it, and whether you are expected to follow a particular style guide or house style.

The complication is that title case is not one universal system. APA, Chicago, MLA, and AP do not all capitalize the same words. Meanwhile, prominent digital style guides from companies such as Google and Microsoft use sentence case for most headings, labels, and interface text.

Use sentence case when you want headings to read naturally and remain easy to scan. Use title case when a publication, academic style, or design convention calls for a traditional display title. Above all, choose a rule and apply it consistently.

Sentence case vs. title case at a glance

Both styles still capitalize proper nouns, acronyms, products, and official names:

What is sentence case?

Sentence case treats a title or heading much like an ordinary sentence.

In sentence case, capitalize:

  1. The first word
  2. Proper nouns, including the names of people, places, organizations, brands, and products
  3. Acronyms and other terms that are normally capitalized
  4. In many title and heading systems, the first word after a colon

Everything else is normally lowercase.

Examples include:

  • How to improve your writing
  • A beginner’s guide to APA Style
  • Writing better headlines with Microsoft Word
  • Capitalization guide: When to use title case

Sentence case does not mean forcing every word after the first into lowercase. Brand names and proper nouns keep their normal capitalization.

Incorrect: How to use iphone in new york

Correct: How to use iPhone in New York

Google and Microsoft both recommend sentence case for most documentation titles and headings. Their guidance also says that headings normally do not need a period at the end.

What is title case?

Title case capitalizes the words considered important in a title. It is also called headline-style capitalization or title-style capitalization.

Most title-case systems agree on a common foundation:

Examples include:

  • How to Write a Better Blog Post
  • The Lord of the Rings
  • A Guide to Grammar: What Every Writer Should Know

Title case does not mean capitalizing every word.

Incorrect under the major title-case systems: How To Write A Better Blog Post

Correct: How to Write a Better Blog Post

Why title case is more complicated than it looks

The small words cause the big arguments.

Consider this heading:

A walk through the woods with friends

Different capitalization systems produce different results:

This is not merely a technical curiosity. It explains why one editor or capitalization tool might produce:

Gone With the Wind

while another produces:

Gone with the Wind

The first can follow APA-style capitalization because with contains four letters. The second follows systems that leave with lowercase. A result can therefore be correct under one guide and incorrect under another.

Chicago made the subject even more interesting in its 18th edition. It changed its longstanding approach and now generally capitalizes prepositions of five letters or more in titles. Older advice saying that Chicago lowercases every preposition is therefore outdated.

What the major style guides do

APA’s split approach is particularly important for students.

An APA paper title and its headings use title case:

The Effects of Social Media on Student Writing

But the title of a book or article in the reference list is written in sentence case:

The effects of social media on student writing

The capitalization is not inconsistent. The two elements serve different purposes and are governed by different APA rules.

AP is also context-dependent. A newsroom headline is not necessarily capitalized the same way as the title of a book mentioned in the article. That is why “I use AP style” is not, by itself, a complete answer to every capitalization question.

Which style is easier to read?

For long digital headings, sentence case is often the more comfortable choice because it follows the capitalization pattern readers encounter in ordinary prose.

That is not merely a personal preference. Google recommends sentence case in headings and titles to support readability and navigation. Microsoft also recommends sentence-style capitalization for most titles and headings, describing it as more readable and easier to localize.

That does not make title case unreadable. Short title-case headings can be clear and attractive:

  • Choose the Right Writing Tool
  • How to Edit Your First Draft
  • Sentence Case vs. Title Case

The difficulty becomes more noticeable when a heading grows longer:

How to Choose the Right Capitalization Style for Every Heading in a Long Business Report

Compare it with sentence case:

How to choose the right capitalization style for every heading in a long business report

The second version has a calmer rhythm. The first may still be perfectly correct, but the length makes every capitalized word more visually prominent.

The longer the heading, the stronger the case for sentence case.

Does title case look more professional?

Not automatically.

Title case often creates a more formal or traditional impression, while sentence case can feel more conversational. But sentence case is used throughout highly professional documentation from companies such as Google and Microsoft.

Capital letters are not tiny neckties. Adding more of them does not automatically make writing more professional.

Professionalism comes from clarity, consistency, and suitability for the context. A law firm, academic journal, or book publisher might prefer title case because it matches an established convention. A software product or help center might prefer sentence case because it supports a simpler, more direct voice.

Neither choice is inherently smarter or more authoritative.

When should you use sentence case or title case?

Start with any rule you are required to follow. When no rule has been specified, use the following as a practical guide.

Can a title use one style while its subheadings use another?

Yes, provided the pattern is deliberate.

A publication might use:

  • Title case for each article title
  • Sentence case for all section headings
  • Sentence case for captions, buttons, and labels

That is still consistent because each level has a defined rule.

Consistency does not mean every piece of text must use the same capitalization. It means comparable elements must be treated the same way.

What looks careless is random variation at the same level:

  • What Is Sentence Case?
  • Why title case is more complicated
  • Which Style Should You Use?
  • How consistency helps

Choose one system for every heading of the same rank and record the decision in a brief house-style guide.

For WordCounter, the rule could be:

Article titles use title case. All headings within articles use sentence case.

Should you preserve the original capitalization of a title?

Sometimes, but not always.

When reproducing a brand name or informally referring to a page or product, preserving its official capitalization may be appropriate.

Formal citations are different. Citation styles frequently normalize titles according to their own rules.

  • APA reference entries use sentence case for many titles.
  • MLA formats English-language work titles in headline style.
  • Chicago applies its current title-case system.

A title printed entirely in lowercase on a book cover might therefore be recapitalized in a bibliography. Conversely, a heavily capitalized webpage title might be reduced to sentence case in an APA reference.

Follow the citation guide rather than assuming the source’s visual styling must always be copied.

What about all caps?

ALL CAPS is not a third solution to the sentence-case-versus-title-case question. It is a display treatment.

It can work for a very short label, logo, or intentionally forceful design element, but it is a poor default for headings and body text. Mixed-case words have more familiar shapes and are generally easier to scan.

Compare:

HOW TO WRITE A BETTER HEADLINE FOR YOUR NEXT BLOG POST

with:

How to write a better headline for your next blog post

All caps does not remove the need for a capitalization policy. It merely hides the policy.

A simple capitalization checklist

Before publishing a title or heading, ask:

  1. Is there a required style guide or house style?
  2. Is this an article title, section heading, interface label, or formal citation?
  3. Have all proper nouns and brand names retained their correct capitalization?
  4. If I am using title case, which version, APA, Chicago, MLA, AP, or another system?
  5. Have comparable headings been capitalized consistently?
  6. Is the heading so long that sentence case would be easier to scan?
  7. Have I avoided using all caps as a shortcut?

If you cannot name the title-case system you are following, sentence case is often the safer option.

The verdict

Sentence case is a strong default for modern digital headings, interface text, and ordinary communication. It is simple, natural, and difficult to misuse.

Title case remains an excellent choice for books, reports, academic headings, formal display titles, and publications with an established headline style. It offers prominence and tradition, but it requires a named rule set.

“Capitalize the important words” is not enough when APA, Chicago, and MLA disagree about which small words count.

So I am still willing to sit on the fence, but the fence now has labels:

  • Use sentence case for natural reading.
  • Use title case when convention calls for it.
  • Use a style guide when accuracy matters.
  • Use the same rule consistently.
